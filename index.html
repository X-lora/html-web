The UK Soft Drinks Industry Levy

A childhood-obesity policy shaped by research,
public debate and political choices.

Guiding question

How did research shape the levy, and what else influenced the final policy?

Case: UK · Policy announced 2016; implemented 6 April 2018.

Why was intervention needed?From health risk to policy problem02 / 05
Background

Sugary drinks became one focus within the wider childhood-obesity debate.

They are widely sold, can contain a lot of free sugar, and can add many calories when people drink them often.

01

Health evidence

Regular sugary-drink consumption can add a lot of sugar and calories, and is linked to tooth decay and weight gain.

02

Children’s health data

Obesity was measured across England and was more common in poorer communities.

03

A policy problem

Government had to decide how to cut children’s sugar intake without putting all the responsibility on families.

The levy starts from the idea that children’s choices are shaped by what companies make and what shops sell.

DHSC (2016), Childhood obesity: a plan for action · Rogers et al. (2023), PLOS Medicine

What did the policy do?Design for reformulation03 / 05

A levy on manufacturers to encourage lower-sugar drinks.

MARCH 2016

Announced

The Chancellor announced the levy as part of the childhood-obesity strategy.

APRIL 2018

Implemented

Manufacturers and importers of eligible sugary drinks started paying the levy.

HOW IT WORKS

Lower the sugar content

Companies could avoid the levy by bringing a drink below a sugar threshold.

18p / litre5g to <8g total sugar per 100ml
24p / litre8g+ total sugar per 100ml

It asked the drinks industry to change products instead of leaving the financial problem only to parents and children.

HM Treasury (2018), SDIL comes into effect · Rates shown are those at implementation.

Why target companies?Changing the food environment04 / 05

The levy focused on the people who decide what goes into drinks.

Why not rely entirely on children or parents?

Why charge manufacturers?

  • Children see what is cheap, easy to buy and heavily advertised.
  • Advice cannot change what is on offer in every family’s local food environment.
  • This approach can leave families carrying most of the responsibility for a problem they do not fully control.
  • Companies decide recipes and can reduce sugar to fall below the levy thresholds.
  • High-sugar drinks become more expensive for firms to sell, without being banned.
  • Firms can reformulate, absorb some of the cost or raise prices. Their responses will differ.
Design principle:

Give companies a reason to sell lower-sugar drinks.

HMRC & HM Treasury (2016), consultation and response · The policy process also involved advocates, industry consultation and political choices.

What can we conclude?Evaluation with caution05 / 05

The levy changed drinks, but it cannot solve obesity inequalities on its own.

46%

average reduction in sugar content in drinks covered by the levy, 2015 to 2020.

−1.6 pp

fall in obesity prevalence among Year 6 girls associated with the levy in one time-series study.

Read the evidence carefully

  • The study cannot show that the levy was the only cause of the change.
  • It found no overall change for younger children or for Year 6 boys.
  • Food prices, poverty and local food environments still shape obesity inequality.

Research made the levy more persuasive. Public pressure, commercial interests and political judgement still shaped the final policy.

HMT & HMRC (2023), SDIL review · Rogers et al. (2023), PLOS Medicine. pp = percentage points.
