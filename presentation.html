An Outline and Evaluation of
AI-Based Policy Simulation

Good policy can still cause harm — simulation lets us test a policy before we launch it. But what if the test itself is only a guess?

The motivation

Good policy can still cause harm

A minimum-wage rise meant to help low-paid workers earn more — yet some small businesses cannot absorb the cost, so they cut staff.

The workers the policy was meant to protect are the ones who lose their jobs. Good intentions are not enough.

Test policies before we launch them.

Neumark et al. (2014)
Introduction3 / 15
The mechanism

Simulation: policy → decision → outcome

A policy changes an individual's circumstances · that person makes a decision · thousands of decisions interact into a social outcome that tells us whether the policy works.

The hard part is the middle link — how each person decides.

Introduction4 / 15
The traditional approach

One fare rise, three reactions

A ten per cent fare rise. A traditional model applies one fixed rule to everyone: −5% ridership across the board.

But real people respond differently:

a car owner may drive · a low-income worker may pay more · a nearby walker may walk.

One rule, one number — real people give three answers.

Introduction5 / 15
The AI approach

AI models each person

Instead of one rule for everyone, each person gets their own AI agent — and its brain is an LLM.

Each agent is given a profile of its person — income, location, but also habits, preferences, personality traits. It weighs that whole bundle of factors to decide, as a real person would.

That sounds ideal — but the agent works by predicting, not by knowing.

Introduction6 / 15
The catch

How the LLM predicts — it does not know

The principle is simple. The LLM reads a sentence, then predicts the most likely next word — it assigns a probability to each candidate and picks the highest.

It does not look up a correct answer; it generates the most likely-sounding one.

So its decision is always a guess, never a fact.

The consequence

A small guess grows down the chain

That guess does not stay contained. A small error at the decision step feeds into the rest of the chain.

And it grows at every step after it. If the first link is a guess, every link after it inherits — and magnifies — that uncertainty.

The problem8 / 15
The real problem

Nobody checked

Where does it break down? Nobody has checked whether the AI's prediction is correct.

Researchers set up the AI, give it a scenario, record its response — but never compare it with real data. They say they will check it later, and move on.

The problem9 / 15
The evidence

15 of 35 studies self-judged

A review by Larooij and Törnberg confirms this pattern. Out of 35 studies they surveyed, 15 relied only on the researchers' own judgement to say whether the simulation was accurate.

Some studies even used an AI to judge another AI. That is circular reasoning: the judge has the same blind spots as the system it is judging.

The response10 / 15
What researchers tried

Three approaches — none checked the decision

GPLab — agents with memory and emotion, weighing options not following rules.

Split model — the LLM decides individually; a separate model handles interaction.

Self-report agents — built from real people's own survey answers.

Each has a strength. But none solves the core problem — not one compared with what real passengers did.

The evaluation11 / 15
The fix

An evaluation system for the decision step

If none checked the decision, what would fix it? An evaluation system for the decision step.

Two aims — verify whether the simulation is accurate; and where it is not, pinpoint the root cause and iterate.

Three components — benchmark, rubric, mechanism. The fare rise shows how they fit together.

The evaluation12 / 15
Component 1

① Benchmark — compare with real data

First, run the fare rise in the simulation — the agents predict, say, a five per cent drop.

The benchmark compares that prediction against the real ridership records from the last actual fare change.

Does the prediction match what actually happened? That is the first check.

The evaluation13 / 15
Component 2

② Rubric — the right people

But matching the total is not enough — that is where the rubric comes in. The drop has to fall on the right people.

If the agents say car owners switched to driving, but the real records show the drop came from low-income riders instead, the simulation has the right number for the wrong reason, and it fails.

The evaluation14 / 15
Component 3

③ Mechanism — fix the agent, run again

Third, the mechanism: find the agent that mis-decided, adjust it, and run again.

Each round moves the prediction closer to the real records.

So together — benchmark, rubric, mechanism — the system checks the decision step against real data, and where it falls short, fixes it. But no system is perfect, and this one has its limits.

Conclusion
Explore ≠ predict

AI-based policy simulation can explore how people might respond to a policy, but it cannot yet predict what will happen. To get closer to prediction, the individual-decision step needs to be checked against real data and improved where it falls short.

Validation needs past data — for a genuinely novel policy none exists, but calibrating where data does exist carries over. The deeper limit: the system checks only the decision step, not the whole chain. And any model used in policy must state openly what it assumes and how it was validated — so others can check its conclusions rather than take them on trust.
