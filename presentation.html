Introduction
The motivation · a case

Good policy can still cause harm

A minimum-wage case

A regional government raised the minimum wage so low-paid workers could earn more — but some small businesses could not absorb the cost, so they let workers go.

The low-wage employees the policy was meant to help ended up not earning more — they lost their jobs instead.

So before a government launches a policy, no one can know what effects and problems it will bring. Sometimes a policy meant to do people good ends up harming them. To reduce that risk, governments can run a policy simulation before launching it.

Neumark et al. (2014)

The mechanism

Simulation: policy → decision → outcome

A policy changes a person's circumstances, which shapes their decision, which guides their behaviour; behaviours interact to produce a social phenomenon we can observe.

The decision step in the middle is the weak link.

Ghaffarzadegan et al. (2024)
Introduction
The traditional approach

One fare rise, one fixed rule

The traditional approach is, in essence, a fixed set of rules: every 10% fare rise cuts passengers within 10 km by 5%, beyond 10 km by 2%. But if your fare went up, would you still ride? It depends on a bike, the rain, even how lazy or afraid of driving you are — something a fixed rule cannot capture.

Ghaffarzadegan et al. (2024); Jamali & Lazarova-Molnar (2026)

The AI approach

AI models each person

An agent stores and combines a person's memory, background, and personality — so it can think and decide like an independent person. But its brain, the LLM, can never be 100% accurate.

Zhang, Peng & Ren (2026)

The catch

How the LLM predicts — it does not know

At each step the LLM picks the token with the highest probability — but the most likely answer is not always the correct one.

Ji et al. (2023)

A guess, not a fact

Tom can sit on Jerry. An LLM only outputs the most likely result — but as situations differ, it will inevitably make mistakes.

The consequence

A small guess grows down the chain

A small error at the decision step is amplified again and again, until the social outcome deviates completely.

The evaluation
The fix

An evaluation system for the decision step

No one has checked whether the decisions AI agents produce match how real people behave. So this essay argues for an evaluation system built for that decision step — with two aims, and a loop that works like an exam.

1Verify whether the decision simulation is accurate.
2Where it is not, pinpoint the root cause and iterate until it meets the standard.

Benchmark, rubric, mechanism — the three parts the next slides unpack. The diagram shows the whole loop.

Chang et al. (2024); Belfrage et al. (2024)
The evaluation
Component 1

① Benchmark — diverse agents

The benchmark is the set of agents we test — the "students" who take the exam. Each agent stands in for one real person, carrying that person's personality, experience, and memory.

In the fare-rise case, the question is the policy: bus fares rise 10% — what would you do? Each benchmark agent gives its own decision, and we compare that against the real ridership records from the last fare change.

Requirement: the agents must be as diverse as possible — different types of people, with different traits, experiences, and memories — so the benchmark captures real individual differences, not one average response.

The evaluation
Component 2

② Rubric — the marking scheme

The rubric is the marking scheme — criteria, set by domain experts, for what a realistic decision looks like in this policy area.

A decision earns marks only if it fits the person behind the agent — their personality, their experience. A plausible-sounding answer that doesn't match the person gets a low score.

[ FIGURE ] Rubric examples for the fare-rise case: a decision that fits the person (e.g. someone whose company is far and who can’t drive keeps the bus) scores high; one that clashes with the person (e.g. a lazy but wealthy person walks a long way) scores low.
Belfrage et al. (2024)
The evaluation
Component 3

③ Mechanism — grade together, diagnose, feed back

The mechanism is how each decision gets marked — and the key question is why two graders work side by side.

The LLM brings efficiency: it screens every agent's decision at scale and catches obvious errors fast.

The human expert brings understanding: whether a decision is realistic depends on the policy's context — knowledge the LLM lacks — so the expert judges the hard, ambiguous cases.

But both do more than score: each writes a diagnosis — where and why it's wrong — which feeds back to the agent. It adjusts, we run again, closer each round, until the score clears the threshold.

[ FIGURE ] The mechanism: LLM (efficiency, screens at scale) + human expert (understanding, judges the hard cases) → both write a diagnosis, not just a score → feedback to the agent → run again, closer each round, until the score clears the threshold.
Li, Das & Shirado (2025); Belfrage et al. (2024)
Conclusion
Conclusion

Explore ≠ predict

The arc of this talk
1
Good policy can harm — so we simulate before we launch.
2
AI agents capture the person — but the LLM's guess at the decision step is never certain.
3
So an evaluation system — benchmark, rubric, mechanism — checks each decision and feeds the weakness back.
AI-based policy simulation can explore how people might respond…
…but it cannot yet predict what will actually happen — so we cannot rely on it completely.
For now it is a tool to explore, not a crystal ball to predict — and the work ahead is to push that line.
Limit 1 · people
It needs people who understand both the AI and the policy area — and those are scarce.
Limit 2 · cost
Scoring each decision takes people, resources, and time — not a one-off, but every round.
Looking ahead
Lighter grading, agents that learn with less — pushing explore toward predict.
Belfrage et al. (2024); Li, Das & Shirado (2025)
